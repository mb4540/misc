Student Introduction

If there is one procedure that is Jemba9's business model, this is it. Spacify™ is not a marketing phrase — it is the design and development process defined in QP-004. Every time Jemba9 takes a proven commercial computer, controller, data router, power supply, robotic mechanism, or sensor and turns it into something that can fly, it happens through the sequence in this procedure: contract review, a requirements baseline, design planning, PDR, CDR, verification, validation, and design release. This topic teaches that sequence end to end.

The core idea to internalize is the difference between two words that sound similar but mean very different things. Verification asks "did we build the design right?" — does the drawing, the BOM, the firmware match what the requirements said, confirmed by analysis, inspection, demonstration, or test, and tracked line-by-line in the Design Verification Matrix (QF-013). Validation asks a harder question: "did we build the right thing?" — will this product actually survive and perform in the real mission environment (thermal extremes, vibration, radiation, vacuum) and meet the customer's actual intended use, not just the words in the spec. That is captured in the Design Validation Plan (QF-014) and proven out through Acceptance Test Procedures and First Article Inspection (AS9102) on representative production units — not on lab prototypes. A commercial part can pass every verification check on paper and still fail validation if nobody proved it survives launch loads or thermal cycling in vacuum. That gap is exactly what a rigorous Spacify™ adaptation is built to close.

The Requirements Traceability Matrix (QF-008) is the connective tissue that makes this whole adaptation defensible to a customer or auditor: it traces every requirement — whether it originated in the customer's SOW, an industry standard, ITAR/EAR or FAR/DFARS regulation, or a Jemba9-added margin for reliability — forward to a specific design output (a drawing, a BOM line in Cetec ERP, a firmware tag) and a specific verification method and result. That traceability is what lets Jemba9 say, credibly, "this commercial-grade part is now a space-qualified part" with evidence behind the claim, not just a change of label.

Design reviews (PDR and CDR) are the formal checkpoints where the Chief Engineer, Quality Manager, Program Manager, Systems Engineer, and often the customer confirm the design is ready to proceed, using entry/exit criteria and documented in Design Review Minutes (QF-007). CDR is the pivotal moment: it is where the **product baseline** is established. Once that baseline exists, it passes out of QP-004's hands and into Configuration Management (QP-002) — every subsequent change, however minor, becomes an ECR/ECO under QP-002, not a free-form design edit. Program records, D&D plans, RTMs, and verification/validation data all live in SharePoint program folders, and released BOMs live at their controlled revision in Cetec ERP.

Who needs this training: Chief Engineer and design engineering staff, Systems Engineers, Program Managers, the Quality Manager, and Manufacturing Manager (for producibility input at PDR/CDR).

Syllabus & Training Goals

Class Syllabus

  1. The design and development lifecycle end to end: contract review → requirements baseline → design planning → PDR → CDR → verification → validation → design release → post-release change control → lessons learned
  2. Customer communication and requirements capture (8.2): distinguishing customer-stated, customer-implied, statutory/regulatory (ITAR/EAR, FAR/DFARS), and Jemba9-determined requirements, and how Contract Review resolves proposal-vs-contract gaps before commitment
  3. Building and maintaining the Requirements Traceability Matrix (QF-008): requirement types, source references, allocation to design outputs, and verification status tracking
  4. The Design and Development Plan: design stages, review schedule, verification/validation methods, interfaces (ICDs), resources, and initial risk entries feeding the Program Risk Register
  5. Design inputs (8.3.3) specific to space hardware: functional, physical, environmental (temperature, vibration, radiation, shock), reliability/life, interface, safety/regulatory, and producibility requirements
  6. Design reviews in practice: PDR and CDR entry/exit criteria, what gets decided at each, and how Design Review Minutes (QF-007) document decisions and action items
  7. Verification vs. validation: the Design Verification Matrix (QF-013) methods (analysis, inspection, demonstration, test) versus the Design Validation Plan (QF-014), ATP, and AS9102 First Article Inspection on representative production units
  8. Design release and the handoff to Configuration Management: what "product baseline" means, and how minor/major/safety-critical changes are controlled afterward under QP-002
  9. Lessons Learned (QF-016) and how program lessons feed forward into future D&D Plans and Management Review (QP-011)

Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, participants will be able to:

  • Explain, in plain terms, how the Spacify™ adaptation model maps onto the ten-step QP-004 lifecycle
  • Correctly distinguish design verification from design validation and give a Jemba9-relevant example of each
  • Locate a requirement in the RTM (QF-008) and trace it forward to its design output and verification result
  • Describe the entry and exit criteria for PDR and CDR and identify what "product baseline" means at CDR
  • Identify which document governs a design change once a baseline is released (QP-002) versus before release (QP-004)
  • State where D&D program records are stored (SharePoint program folders; Cetec ERP for released BOMs)

Recommended audience:

Chief Engineer Design / Systems Engineers Program Managers Quality Manager Manufacturing Manager

Suggested periodicity: Full session at role onboarding for engineering and program staff; refresher review at the start of each new program kickoff and whenever QP-004 is revised.
